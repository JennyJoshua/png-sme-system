# PNG SME Operations and Latent Business Needs Research

**Status:** Desk-research handover (no primary interviews conducted).  
**Date prepared:** 23 October 2026  
**Geographic focus:** Papua New Guinea, with explicit attention to Port Moresby, Lae, provincial towns, district towns and rural/remote areas.  
**Purpose:** Identify evidence-based operational problems, latent needs, existing solutions and commercially plausible opportunity areas for further validation. This report does **not** assume that software is the solution and does not recommend building a product yet.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Papua New Guinea’s MSME sector is dominated by very small, often informal, cash-based businesses operating under constrained infrastructure, limited access to finance, and uneven access to suppliers, customers and business-support services. The strongest current evidence base is the 2024 *PNG Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises and Informal Sector Study*, commissioned through the Australia–PNG Economic Partnership and conducted by Tebbutt Research; it surveyed 1,309 formal MSMEs and 1,302 informal businesses across 21 provinces. [web:1][web:3][web:12]

The clearest **documented** constraints are:

- **Access to finance and credit.** In the 2024 study, 31% of formal MSMEs described access to finance, loans and capital as a major obstacle, with a further 25% describing it as a minor obstacle; 56% reported difficulty obtaining a loan. [web:3][web:6]
- **Security, crime and law-and-order risk.** This was a major obstacle for 34% of formal MSMEs in 2024, up from 21% in 2014. [web:3]
- **Unfair competitive practices.** Reported as a major obstacle by 33% of formal MSMEs in 2024. [web:3]
- **Cash reliance and weak financial-service usage.** PNG MSMEs and households rely heavily on cash; low account usage, limited financial literacy, connectivity constraints and trust issues limit digital financial services. [web:5][web:10]
- **Formalisation intent is high but conversion is uncertain.** Among informal businesses, 21% said they were very likely and 40% somewhat likely to register within 12 months, but the study also identifies documentation and finance barriers. [web:3]
- **The sector is overwhelmingly micro-scale.** Formal MSMEs surveyed averaged 7.0 paid employees; 67% were micro, 27% small and only 6% medium enterprises. Informal businesses were even smaller: 91% reported annual turnover below PGK 60,000. [web:4][web:3]

The most defensible initial opportunity areas are therefore **not** “build an SME app” but narrowly defined services that reduce a frequent, costly operational failure and can be tested with a small number of paying customers. The strongest hypotheses are:

1. **Customer-credit and receivables tracking** for trade stores, wholesalers, service businesses and transport operators.
2. **Simple quotation, invoice and job-record support** for contractors, tradespeople, mechanics and professional service providers.
3. **Business-record preparation to improve loan readiness**, delivered as a human-supported service rather than a self-serve app.
4. **Supplier price/availability coordination** in specific supply chains, particularly building materials, agricultural inputs and fresh produce.
5. **Tender-readiness and business-document services** for formal SMEs seeking government or corporate contracts.

Each remains a **commercial validation required** opportunity: the evidence establishes that related problems exist, but not that owners will pay, at what price, through which channel, or with sufficient retention.

## 2. Scope and Research Methodology

### 2.1 Approach

This report uses a structured desk-research approach:

1. Establish the current national evidence base on PNG MSMEs.
2. Extract sector-wide findings from official and commissioned studies.
3. Map operating models and pain points by business category.
4. Test the supplied latent-need hypotheses against available evidence.
5. Identify existing solutions, informal workarounds and competitors.
6. Assess opportunity areas with explicit uncertainty labels.
7. Recommend a first round of business-owner interviews.

### 2.2 Evidence hierarchy

Priority was given to:

1. PNG government and statutory sources.
2. The 2024 PNG MSME and Informal Sector Study commissioned by the Australian Government.
3. National statistical and research publications.
4. Multilateral and development-sector research.
5. Reputable PNG business reporting and sector publications.

Where original full-text data was not inspected in this pass, findings are labelled accordingly and should not be treated as fully verified.

### 2.3 Evidence labels

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **DOCUMENTED FINDING** | Directly supported by an inspected credible source. |
| **REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS** | Plausible and partly supported, but not directly established for the specified population. |
| **UNVERIFIED ASSUMPTION** | Plausible idea without sufficient supporting evidence found in this research pass. |
| **FIELD VALIDATION REQUIRED** | Cannot be reliably answered without interviews or observation. |
| **COMMERCIAL VALIDATION REQUIRED** | Willingness to pay, adoption, retention or unit economics remain unknown. |

### 2.4 Key limitation

This is **desk research only**. No PNG business owners, employees, suppliers, lenders or government officers were interviewed. Claims about day-to-day workflows—such as how a particular trade store records credit, how a Lae hardware retailer compares supplier prices, or how a rural aggregator coordinates transport—require primary validation.

## 3. Structure of the PNG MSME Sector

### 3.1 Population and composition

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** The 2024 national study surveyed 2,611 businesses: 1,309 formal MSMEs and 1,302 informal businesses, across 21 provinces and 61 fieldwork locations. It also included 41 qualitative depth interviews. [web:12][web:1]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** Among surveyed formal MSMEs, 67% were micro-enterprises, 27% small enterprises and 6% medium enterprises. Formal MSMEs employed an average of 7.0 paid employees and 2.7 unpaid staff. [web:4]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** Informal businesses are typically micro-scale: 91% reported annual turnover below PGK 60,000, compared with 27% of formal MSMEs reporting turnover in the PGK 60,000–249,999 range. [web:3][web:4]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** The practical target market for low-cost business services is therefore concentrated in micro and small enterprises, many of which have limited administrative capacity and low tolerance for fixed monthly software fees.

**UNVERIFIED ASSUMPTION:** Exact national counts of all MSMEs and informal enterprises remain uncertain. Older sources cite approximately 49,500 registered MSMEs, while recent reporting refers to around 550,000 registered enterprises; these figures use different definitions and should not be merged. [web:11][web:15]

### 3.2 Formal versus informal operations

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** The 2024 study treats formal MSMEs and informal businesses as distinct survey populations. Informal businesses were active in more than 12 industries and formal MSMEs in more than 16 industries. [web:2][web:3]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** A majority of surveyed informal operators expressed intent to formalise: 21% said they were very likely and 40% somewhat likely to register within the next 12 months. [web:3]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Formalisation intent does not by itself prove demand for registration services. The binding constraints may include cost, documentation, tax uncertainty, lack of perceived benefit, and limited access to business-support advice.

**FIELD VALIDATION REQUIRED:** What specifically prevents informal owners who say they intend to register from doing so?

### 3.3 Finance and banking

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** Access to loans, credit and capital is a leading obstacle. In the 2024 study, 31% called it a major obstacle and 25% a minor obstacle; 13% of MSME loans were facilitated through informal lenders. [web:3]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** More than half of surveyed enterprises—56%—experienced difficulty obtaining a loan, including 31% for whom it was a major obstacle. [web:6]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** A PNG National Research Institute discussion paper found that 75% of respondents agreed it was difficult to access government-funded SME loans, while 66% agreed it was difficult to deal with banks. This was a smaller, partly online sample and should not be treated as nationally representative. [web:9]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Finance constraints are not caused only by lack of collateral. They also reflect documentation gaps, limited financial records, bank-process friction, perceived high risk, and limited awareness of suitable products.

**COMMERCIAL VALIDATION REQUIRED:** Whether owners will pay for loan-readiness, bookkeeping or document-preparation support, and whether improved records materially change lending outcomes.

### 3.4 Digital access and payments

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** Cash remains central to MSME transactions. UN Capital Development Fund reporting notes low utilisation of bank accounts even among those who open them, heavy cash reliance, limited financial and digital literacy, and mobile-phone access constraints—particularly for women. [web:5]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** Pacific financial-inclusion discussions identify mobile coverage, connectivity failures, agent liquidity, training, customer protection and low rural mobile-money acceptance as continuing barriers. [web:10]

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** A World Bank mobile-money report highlights limited mobile coverage, connectivity failures, phone-charging difficulties and agent float constraints as major barriers to reliable digital financial services. [web:7]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Any digital business tool must work under intermittent connectivity, support low-end Android phones, avoid dependence on continuous data, and accommodate cash and mobile-money payment paths.

### 4.1 Cross-cutting operating reality

**DOCUMENTED FINDING:** MSMEs operate across urban, rural and remote locations and supply essential goods and services; the 2024 study specifically identifies their role in community-level access to goods, services and employment. [web:2][web:3]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Business models must be segmented by location and business type. A Port Moresby wholesaler with reliable power, bank access and staff may be able to use conventional cloud software; a rural trade store or produce aggregator may need offline-first, low-data, human-assisted or agent-supported service delivery.

## 6. Financial and Cash-Flow Challenges

### 6.1 What is documented

- **Access to finance is a leading constraint.** 31% of formal MSMEs called access to finance a major obstacle; another 25% called it minor. [web:3]
- **Loan difficulty is widespread.** 56% of surveyed enterprises reported difficulty getting a loan. [web:6]
- **Informal finance fills part of the gap.** 13% of MSME loans were facilitated through informal lenders. [web:3]
- **Cash dominance limits financial visibility.** Heavy reliance on cash, low account utilisation and limited financial skills are documented in development-sector research. [web:5]
- **Financial literacy and digital capability are uneven.** Low numeracy/literacy, limited training access and digital-confidence issues constrain adoption of financial services. [web:5][web:10]

### 6.2 Hypothesis assessment

| Hypothesis | Assessment | Evidence | Next test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owners cannot easily determine actual profit | **REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS** | Cash reliance and weak financial capability are documented; direct measurement of profit-calculation failure is not established in this pass [web:5] | Ask owners to reconstruct last week’s sales, costs, credit and profit; observe records |
| Customer credit creates hidden cash-flow problems | **REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS** | Consistent with cash-based operations and finance constraints, but no PNG-specific credit-ledger evidence inspected | Map a real credit book: balances, aging, partial payments, write-offs |
| Weak records contribute to loan rejection | **REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS** | Informal businesses often lack documentation needed for loans; bank friction is documented [web:3][web:9] | Interview lenders and rejected applicants; test a record-preparation pilot |
| Owners want dashboards | **UNVERIFIED ASSUMPTION** | No evidence inspected that owners want or would use dashboards | Ask for the last decision where better information would have changed behaviour |

### 6.3 Commercial implication

A promising service may be **“business records that unlock money”**: simple sales, expense, stock and customer-debt records packaged with a practical loan-readiness output. This should be sold as a outcome-linked or fixed-fee service first, not as a general accounting app.

**COMMERCIAL VALIDATION REQUIRED:** Price sensitivity, retention, data-entry burden, trust, and whether lenders value the resulting records.

## 7. Sales, Customer and Order Management

### 7.1 Likely problems

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Missed enquiries, unclear quotations, delayed follow-up and weak customer records are plausible constraints for contractors, mechanics, professional services, hardware suppliers and wholesalers. However, this research pass did not inspect PNG-specific evidence quantifying these losses.

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** WhatsApp and phone calls are likely important commercial channels, but the claim that “most PNG SMEs use WhatsApp to manage orders” is **not established** by the evidence reviewed here.

## 8. Inventory, Purchasing and Supplier Challenges

### 8.1 What can be inferred

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Stock-outs, overstocking, spoilage, price changes and supplier unreliability are plausible pain points, especially for perishable-food traders, agricultural aggregators, hardware retailers and small wholesalers.

**DOCUMENTED CONTEXT:** Transport of goods, access to finance and supplier/bank-related difficulties have historically constrained MSMEs, although the 2024 study reports some improvement in these areas relative to 2014. [web:3]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Rural and provincial businesses face higher supplier-search and logistics costs because suppliers, banks, professional services and business support are more concentrated in urban centres. [web:3][web:5]

### 8.2 Potential opportunity: supplier coordination

A supplier-discovery or price-comparison service could address fragmented supplier information, but it faces a classic marketplace cold-start problem: it needs enough buyers and suppliers simultaneously.

| Criterion | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Problem evidence | REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS; not yet directly quantified in PNG |
| Target customers | Hardware retailers, trade stores, food businesses, agri-traders, contractors |
| Frequency | Potentially weekly or per procurement cycle; unverified |
| Severity | Potentially high where stock-outs stop sales or projects |
| Current workaround | Phone calls, personal networks, travelling to suppliers |
| Existing competition | Informal broker relationships, WhatsApp groups, direct supplier sales reps |
| Willingness to pay | UNKNOWN |
| Reachability | Moderate in urban clusters; harder in rural areas |
| Technology fit | Possible, but a human concierge may be more viable initially |
| Connectivity fit | Offline lists, SMS, WhatsApp and call-based workflows may be needed |
| Delivery cost | High if the operator verifies prices and availability manually |
| Revenue model | Supplier listing, success fee, subscription, procurement commission |
| Validation method | Concierge test: find best price/availability for 10 real purchase requests |

**COMMERCIAL VALIDATION REQUIRED:** Whether buyers will pay for verified supplier information, and whether suppliers will maintain accurate listings.

## 11. Staff and Business Management

### 11.1 What is plausible

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Owner dependence is likely high in micro-enterprises because formal MSMEs average only 7 paid employees and informal businesses are much smaller. [web:4][web:3]

**REASONABLE HYPOTHESIS:** Common management issues may include attendance, task allocation, stock shrinkage, cash handling, training and delegation. However, no PNG-specific evidence inspected in this pass quantifies staff-related losses by business type.

### 11.2 Potential services

- Simple staff attendance and task sheets.
- Cash-handling and end-of-day reconciliation routines.
- Job cards for repairs, construction and service work.
- Owner-facing daily summaries prepared by a bookkeeper or business-support agent.
- Training delivered through existing business associations, market groups or church/community networks.

**FIELD VALIDATION REQUIRED:** Which staff and management problems owners rank as costly, and whether they would pay for a service rather than manage informally.

## 12. Technology, Connectivity and Digital Adoption

### 12.1 Documented constraints

| Constraint | Evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Cash preference and low account usage | UNCDF reports heavy cash reliance and low utilisation of bank accounts even after opening [web:5] | Digital tools must support cash-first workflows |
| Digital literacy and confidence gaps | Low financial/digital literacy and confidence constraints documented [web:5][web:10] | Human onboarding and simple interfaces are likely necessary |
| Mobile access inequality | Women’s phone ownership lags; many access phones through social networks [web:5] | Do not assume each owner has a dedicated smartphone |
| Connectivity and power | Coverage, connectivity failures, charging and agent float constraints documented [web:7][web:10] | Offline-first or low-data design may be necessary |
| Technology perceived as difficult | SMEs report limited understanding of mobile technology and continued cash use [web:10] | Assisted setup, local language support and trust-building matter |

### 12.2 Design implication

If a digital tool is eventually considered, the likely minimum viable form is:

- Android-first.
- Works offline and syncs when connectivity is available.
- Supports cash, mobile money and bank-transfer records.
- Uses minimal data entry and local examples.
- Includes human onboarding through an agent, bookkeeper, association or retail partner.
- Has a clear immediate payback, such as recovering a debt, winning a quote, or preparing loan records.

This is a design hypothesis, not a recommendation to begin development.

### 16.2 What not to conclude

- A documented national constraint does **not** prove a paying market.
- High formalisation intent does **not** prove demand for registration assistance.
- Mobile-money availability does **not** prove that SMEs will adopt a new digital workflow.
- A software gap does **not** mean software is the right first product; a human-delivered concierge service may test demand faster and more cheaply.

## 18. Major Evidence Gaps and Uncertainties

1. **No primary business-owner interviews have been conducted.** All workflow claims require field validation.
2. **Willingness to pay is unknown** for every proposed opportunity.
3. **Customer-acquisition cost is unknown**, especially outside Port Moresby and Lae.
4. **The actual frequency and financial cost of credit defaults, stock-outs, spoilage and missed quotations is not quantified** in the evidence inspected.
5. **The full 2024 MSME study dataset and detailed tables were not exhaustively analysed in this pass.** The report and factsheet establish major findings, but category-level and provincial-level analysis should be extracted from the full document before opportunity selection.
6. **Current registration, tax, licensing and procurement requirements need verification from official PNG sources** before any compliance-related service is offered.
7. **Lender perspectives are missing.** It is unknown which records most influence SME credit decisions in PNG.
8. **Digital adoption behaviour is not well established at business-workflow level.** National constraints are documented, but specific tool usage by trade stores, contractors and transport operators is not.
9. **Geographic unit economics are unknown.** A service viable in Port Moresby may fail in a district town due to travel, support and customer-density costs.
10. **Informal workarounds may be adequate.** A free notebook or trusted informal network can defeat a paid tool even where a problem exists.

## 19. Recommended Business-Owner Interview Plan

### 19.1 Sample: 10 owners

| # | Business type | Suggested location | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trade store | Port Moresby settlement or peri-urban area | Cash, credit, stock and supplier workflow |
| 2 | Trade store | Lae or another provincial town | Compare urban/provincial credit and supply practices |
| 3 | Hardware/building-material retailer | Port Moresby or Lae | Quotes, contractor customers, stock, supplier terms |
| 4 | Small contractor or tradesperson | Port Moresby | Quoting, labour/material costing, payment tracking |
| 5 | Transport owner-operator | Lae or Port Moresby | Bookings, trip costs, fuel, maintenance, collections |
| 6 | Mechanic/repair business | Provincial town | Job cards, parts sourcing, estimates, repeat customers |
| 7 | Fresh-produce market vendor | Port Moresby market | Purchasing, perishability, transport, daily cash |
| 8 | Small food business/restaurant | Port Moresby or Lae | Ingredients, waste, pricing, staff, daily sales |
| 9 | Agricultural producer or aggregator | A producing province accessible from the pilot base | Buyers, aggregation, transport, spoilage, payment |
| 10 | Professional/service business | Port Moresby | Invoicing, receivables, scheduling, client records |

Aim for a mix of women- and men-owned businesses, formal and informal operators, and at least three businesses outside Port Moresby where feasible.

### 19.2 Interview method

- Conduct 45–60 minute interviews at the business premises where safe and appropriate.
- Obtain verbal informed consent; explain purpose, voluntary participation, confidentiality and right to stop.
- Do not request bank statements, tax records, customer lists or exact revenue figures unless essential and explicitly consented.
- Focus on recent, specific events rather than hypothetical preferences.
- Record structured notes; audio-record only with explicit permission.
- Avoid leading questions such as: “Would you use an app that solves this problem?”

### 19.3 Core questions

1. Walk me through how your business operated yesterday.
2. How did you record the sales you made?
3. How do you know how much profit the business made last week?
4. Tell me about the last time you ran out of stock unexpectedly.
5. How do you decide when and where to buy supplies?
6. What happens when a customer asks to buy on credit?
7. Tell me about the last quotation or invoice you prepared.
8. Which administrative task takes the most time?
9. What was the last business problem that cost you money?
10. How did you handle it?
11. Have you paid anyone to help solve this problem?
12. What tools or services have you tried?
13. What did you dislike about those solutions?
14. If the problem disappeared, what would improve in your business?
15. What would make you trust a new service provider?

### 19.4 Evidence tests

| Hypothesis | Interview test | Confirmation signal | Rejection signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit invisibility | Owner reconstructs last week’s sales, purchases, expenses and profit | Cannot reconstruct accurately; estimates conflict with records | Clear, current records and reliable profit calculation |
| Credit leakage | Review credit book structure and recent overdue cases | Forgotten balances, partial payments, disputes, cash stress | Tight credit policy and accurate tracking |
| Quote inefficiency | Ask owner to describe/recreate last quote | Time-consuming, error-prone, inconsistent documents | Fast, standardised process with follow-up |
| Supplier fragmentation | Ask about last stock purchase decision | Multiple calls, uncertainty, travel, price surprises | Reliable supplier information and predictable pricing |
| Loan-readiness gap | Discuss last loan application or reason for not applying | Missing records, unclear requirements, process frustration | Records are adequate; barrier is collateral, price or risk appetite |
| Rural access constraint | Compare supplier/service trips and costs by location | Frequent travel, high coordination cost, limited local service | Adequate local access and low coordination burden |

## 20. Recommended Next Research Steps

1. **Extract the full 2024 MSME study.** Download and analyse the detailed findings report, especially tables on obstacles, finance, formalisation, business support, geography, gender and digital access. [web:1][web:3][web:4]
2. **Verify official compliance requirements.** Confirm current business-registration, tax, licensing and procurement requirements directly from the relevant PNG agencies before designing any compliance-related offer.
3. **Run 10 owner interviews** using the plan above.
4. **Run one concierge pilot**, preferably customer-credit clean-up, quotation support, or supplier sourcing—whichever emerges most frequently and painfully in interviews.
5. **Charge a small real fee** during the pilot. Free pilots measure interest, not demand.
6. **Interview 3–5 lenders, business associations or business-development providers** to understand what records and documentation they actually require.
7. **Map one supply chain end-to-end** if supplier, freight or agricultural coordination appears strongest.
8. **Only then assess whether a digital tool is necessary.** If a human-assisted service does not generate repeat paid use, an app is unlikely to solve the underlying adoption problem.

## Final handover notes

**Strongest documented findings**

- Finance access is a leading MSME constraint. [web:3][web:6]
- Security/crime and unfair competitive practices are major reported obstacles. [web:3]
- The sector is predominantly micro-scale, with a large informal component. [web:3][web:4]
- Cash reliance, digital-literacy constraints, connectivity and infrastructure limitations shape technology adoption. [web:5][web:7][web:10]
- Rural and remote businesses face weaker access to services and support. [web:3]

**Strongest hypotheses requiring interviews**

- Customer credit creates untracked cash-flow leakage.
- Poor records reduce loan readiness.
- Quotation, invoicing and job documentation are inefficient for service and trade businesses.
- Supplier and freight coordination is costly in provincial and rural settings.
- Owners need practical business records more than generic dashboards.

**Most important unanswered questions**

- Which problem costs owners the most money, time or lost sales?
- Which owners already pay someone to solve a related problem?
- What price, delivery channel and trust mechanism would make a service usable?
- Which locations have enough customer density for a sustainable pilot?
- Do lenders value improved SME records, and which records matter most?

**Next activity:** conduct the 10 structured business-owner interviews and one small paid concierge pilot. Do not begin product design or development until those results are available.
